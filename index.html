The Central Philosophy of Resilience 2035

Strong communities are not created simply by launching programs. They are strengthened by nurturing relationships, honoring collective knowledge, practicing mutual care, connecting existing resources, and creating conditions in which people can build a future together.

The goal is to help communities recognize what they already possess, reconnect what has become disconnected, strengthen what is working, address what is missing, and build new structures only when they are genuinely needed.

Black communities have demonstrated this kind of resilience for generations — often without institutional recognition, adequate funding, or public acknowledgment. This guide exists to name those practices, build on them, and extend their reach through 2035.

Who This Guide Is For

Written for anyone who wants to strengthen their community — with or without formal organization, funding, or credentials.

  • An individual who wants to begin helping in their neighborhood
  • A family checking on neighbors or sharing resources
  • An elder passing on knowledge and mentorship
  • A small group of residents with no formal organization
  • A church, mosque, or faith community
  • An established nonprofit seeking deeper community roots
  • A school, youth program, or local business
  • A rural community with limited services but deep relationships
  • An urban neighborhood with many disconnected organizations that could collaborate

Communities differ across generations, regions, faiths, and circumstances. This guide offers frameworks, not formulas. Adapt what is useful. Leave what is not.

Rooted in What We Already Know

Black communities have never waited for outside institutions to begin caring for one another. Mutual aid, collective giving, kinship caregiving, elder mentorship, cooperative economics — these are not new ideas. They are the traditions that sustained our communities through conditions designed to make survival impossible.

This guide does not ask you to import a corporate framework or professionalize your neighborhood. It asks you to recognize what your community already does, strengthen what is already working, reconnect what has become disconnected, and build new structures only when they are genuinely needed.

You do not need to form a nonprofit to begin. You do not need a grant to hold a conversation. You do not need a title to check on a neighbor. The work of community building is already happening around you. This guide helps you see it, name it, and build on it.

Getting Started

Start Here: Bring People Together Before You Build

Every successful community initiative begins with conversation, not construction. Before launching a program, business, garden, or clinic — you need to know who your neighbors are, what they actually need, and who is already working on it.

The most powerful community infrastructure often has no name, no 501(c)(3), and no office. It is the neighbor who brings food when someone is grieving. The elder who mentors the young man nobody else has time for. The group chat that circulates jobs and resources. The family that quietly covers another family's rent. Recognize these relationships as the foundation — not the gap — before you build anything new.

Step 1: Start a Conversation

You do not need a formal organization, a grant, or a meeting room to begin. You need one conversation — then another.

  • Talk to 5–10 people you already know: neighbors, fellow church members, coworkers, parents at school
  • Ask: "What do you think is the biggest challenge in our neighborhood right now?"
  • Listen more than you talk. Write down what you hear.
  • Look for patterns: three or more people naming the same issue is a signal worth following
  • Do not pitch a solution yet. Your job is to understand the problem as others experience it
How to Hold a Listening Session

An informal gathering of 8–20 residents. Not a town hall, lecture, or recruitment event.

  • Location: A living room, church hall, library, or community center — accessible and free
  • Invitation: Personal invitations work better than flyers
  • Format: Introductions, two to three open questions, everyone speaks, close with "What next?"
  • Notes: Assign someone to capture themes. Share a summary afterward.
  • Follow up: Within one week — thank everyone, share what you heard, suggest a next step
Recognize What Already Exists

Before proposing anything new, recognize the community infrastructure already present — much of it informal, uncounted, and undervalued.

  • Relationships: Extended family networks, kinship ties, chosen family, longtime neighbors
  • Informal caregiving: People checking on elders, sharing childcare, helping with transportation
  • Cultural knowledge: Elders, tradition-keepers, healers, storytellers, community historians
  • Mutual aid: Informal resource sharing, collective giving, neighborhood watch, food sharing
  • Gathering places: Churches, barbershops, beauty salons, parks, corner stores
  • Organizations: Nonprofits, businesses, schools, clubs — formal but not always better-connected
  • Communication networks: Group chats, social media, newsletters, word of mouth

Connecting what already exists is nearly always more effective than building something new from scratch.

Include Everyone
  • Young people: Hold separate youth conversations or create youth-specific roles
  • Older adults: Consider transportation barriers and meeting times. Home visits matter.
  • Renters: Often excluded from neighborhood planning — reach them intentionally
  • Non-church members: Reach people through schools, barber shops, laundromats, parks
  • People with disabilities: Ensure accessible venues and multiple formats
  • Working families: Evening/weekend times and brief meetings increase participation
Form a Working Group

After your conversations, identify 5–10 people willing to stay involved. This is a starting team — not a board, not a nonprofit, not a permanent structure. It can be informal.

  • Aim for diversity: different ages, backgrounds, relationships to the community, and affiliations
  • Include people not attached to any organization — independent voices matter
  • Include elders who carry historical knowledge and young people who will inherit the work
  • Decisions made together — rotating facilitation works well in early stages
  • Agree on how disagreements will be handled before they arise
  • Select one achievable initial project based on what was heard
  • A formal structure (nonprofit, LLC, committee) is only needed when handling money, signing contracts, or applying for grants — not before
Approaching Potential Partners
  • Research organizations already active on the issues your community identified
  • Ask specifically: "We are working on X. Are you? Could we talk?"
  • Offer something: volunteer time, space, referrals, or shared promotion
  • Do not assume a church must lead or a nonprofit must be in charge
  • Document commitments in writing, even just an email summary
  • A "not now" is not a rejection

Getting Started

The 9-Step Community-Building Framework

This framework applies to every topic in this guide. Whether you are building a community garden, a health ministry, a youth program, or a neighborhood safety network — the process is the same. Learn it once. Use it everywhere.

01
LISTEN
Understand residents' experiences, concerns, priorities, and aspirations before proposing solutions.
02
CONNECT
Bring residents, organizations, professionals, and potential partners together around shared concerns.
03
ASSESS
Map existing assets, service gaps, barriers, and available resources before assuming new funding is needed.
04
PRIORITIZE
Select a manageable number of shared goals based on community input, not leader preference.
05
ORGANIZE
Build a team, assign responsibilities, establish decision-making processes, and set deadlines.
06
ACT
Launch a small, practical initiative. Start with what you have.
07
MEASURE
Evaluate participation, outcomes, challenges, and community feedback honestly.
08
SUSTAIN
Develop partnerships, funding strategies, leadership succession, and long-term community ownership.
09
SHARE
Communicate results, recognize contributors, document lessons learned, and invite broader participation.

The most common mistake is skipping from step 1 directly to step 6. Someone has an idea, organizes a project, and wonders why nobody shows up. Steps 2 through 5 are what make step 6 work.

This framework works because it reflects how Black communities have always organized. Mutual aid, community feeding, collective child-rearing, neighborhood watch, rotating savings groups — these were not formal programs. They were relationships with shared responsibility at the center. This guide names those practices as legitimate, powerful, and worth building on.

Key Questions for Each Step

LISTEN: Who is most affected? What do they say the problem is?

CONNECT: Who else cares? Who is already working on it?

ASSESS: What skills, spaces, and organizations already exist?

PRIORITIZE: Which goal has the most community support and is achievable first?

ORGANIZE: Who does what, by when, and how will decisions be made?

ACT: What is the smallest version we can test?

MEASURE: Did it work? Who was reached? What was the cost?

SUSTAIN: Who will own this long-term? How will it be funded?

SHARE: Who should know? What did we learn?

When to Move Slowly
  • If you cannot name five specific people who will participate, you are not ready to launch
  • If the initiative was designed without the people it is meant to serve, it is a service delivery project — not community ownership
  • If you do not have a plan for when the initial leader steps back, you do not yet have a movement — you have a personality
  • Rebuilding trust after a broken relationship or a failed initiative takes longer than building it the first time. Slow down to protect it.
  • A small, successful pilot people believe in is worth more than a large initiative that collapses in year two
  • Spending months building relationships before starting a single program is not slow progress. It is the work.
Getting Started

Your First 90 Days

A realistic sequence for moving from your first conversation to your first community initiative. Time frames are adaptable. What matters is completing each phase with genuine community engagement — not rushing to launch.

Days 1 to 30
Listen and Connect
  • Have individual conversations with 10–20 community members — neighbors, family, church members, people at the barbershop or beauty salon, parents at the school gate
  • Ask about concerns and about what is already working — both matter
  • Notice informal networks already in operation: who helps whom, who people call in a crisis, who knows everyone
  • Hold one or two listening sessions (8–20 people each) in comfortable, accessible locations — a living room is fine
  • Document what you hear: issues mentioned most, resources people already know about, people others keep mentioning
  • Identify organizations and informal networks already addressing the most common concerns
  • Reach out to two or three trusted connectors or existing efforts to introduce yourself
  • Select one issue for further exploration — do not commit to a project yet
Days 31 to 60
Organize and Plan
  • Form a working group of 5–10 people who want to move forward
  • Review what was heard and identify one specific, achievable goal
  • Complete a community asset map: spaces, skills, organizations, and resources that already exist
  • Identify potential partners and have conversations about collaboration
  • Develop a basic plan: what, who, when, at what cost
  • Develop a no-cost or low-cost version in case initial funding is unavailable
  • Decide how success will be evaluated
  • Set a launch date for a small pilot
Days 61 to 90
Launch and Learn
  • Launch the small pilot project with the working group and confirmed partners
  • Communicate clearly to the broader community what it is and how to participate
  • Track participation, activities completed, expenses, and early outcomes
  • Gather feedback: what worked, what did not, what was missing
  • Hold a working group reflection meeting to review results
  • Share results with the broader community, including what will change
  • Decide: continue, improve, expand, or stop
What Success Looks Like at 90 Days
  • Stronger relationships among people who were not previously connected
  • A working group that meets regularly and makes decisions together
  • At least one completed listening session with documented findings shared back
  • At least one pilot activity with real participants
  • At least one partnership or coordination with an existing organization or network
  • An honest collective assessment of what worked, what did not, and what to do next

Restored trust between disconnected people is one of the most significant outcomes a 90-day effort can produce. It may not show up in a grant report, but it is the foundation everything else rests on.

After 90 Days
  • Apply for small grants with a track record to show
  • Recruit additional volunteers and organizational partners
  • Expand successful pilots into sustained programs
  • Apply the same framework to a second community priority
  • Begin developing leadership succession so the work outlasts any one person
Section A / Organize & Lead

Community Organization & Leadership

Effective community change requires relationships before it requires structure. Formal roles and official titles can come later — first, people need to trust each other enough to make decisions together, share resources, and hold one another accountable with care.

There is no single right way to lead. Black communities have organized through elders providing guidance, through rotating facilitation, through consensus circles, through family and neighborhood coordination, and yes, through formal boards and executive leadership. The model that serves the community is the right one — not the model that satisfies a funder, impresses an institution, or resembles a corporation.

Why This Matters

Many community initiatives collapse not because they ran out of funding but because they depended on one or two leaders without developing broader ownership. Community organizing is the discipline of building distributed leadership and institutional structures that outlast individuals.

Who Needs to Be at the Table
  • Longtime residents who understand neighborhood history
  • Young people and new residents who bring fresh perspective
  • People directly affected by the issue — not just people who care about it
  • Representatives of churches, schools, nonprofits, businesses
  • People with facilitation, legal, financial, or technical skills
Meeting Facilitation Basics
  • Circulate an agenda before the meeting
  • Assign a timekeeper and note-taker for every meeting
  • Use a round-robin at the start so no one dominates early
  • Name conflicts: "It sounds like we have two different priorities here. Can we name them?"
  • Document decisions clearly: who decided, what was decided, who is responsible, by when
  • Share meeting notes within 48 hours
Leadership Development
  • Identify emerging leaders early and give them real responsibilities — not just tasks
  • Create mentoring relationships between experienced and newer leaders
  • Rotate facilitation roles so multiple people develop those skills
  • Create a written succession plan before it is needed
  • Celebrate contributions publicly to build a culture of recognition
Building Coalitions
  • Map every organization working on related issues in your area
  • Identify complementary strengths: one org has space, another has staff, another has trust
  • Start with a small, specific collaboration before a formal partnership
  • Agree in writing on decision-making, resource sharing, credit, and communication
  • Revisit the partnership agreement annually
  • No one organization needs to lead everything

  • Word in BlackCollaborative of Black news publishers sharing community-focused journalism.
  • Black Star ProjectCommunity-based organization focused on education and quality of life initiatives.
Community Action Checklist
Individual
  • Identify one community group to join
  • Attend one public meeting this month
  • Introduce yourself to two neighbors
Small Group
  • Hold a listening session
  • Create a shared decision-making process
  • Assign roles with clear responsibilities
Organization
  • Map partner organizations in your area
  • Draft a written partnership agreement
  • Create a leadership development plan
Section B / Economic Power

Economic Development & Local Business

Supporting Black-owned businesses, expanding local purchasing networks, and building community wealth are long-term priorities that require coordination among residents, entrepreneurs, institutions, and financial partners.

Why This Matters

When communities circulate dollars among local businesses, it builds local wealth, creates jobs, and strengthens neighborhood institutions. This requires intentional coordination — not just individual consumer choices.

What We Can Build Together
  • A searchable directory of Black-owned businesses in your area
  • A "Buy Local" campaign coordinated through churches, schools, and social media
  • A small business support network with mentorship and referral systems
  • A cooperative purchasing group for shared supplies and services
  • Connections to Black-focused business development organizations
Start With What We Have
  • Survey your congregation, neighborhood, or network for business owners
  • Map existing Black-owned businesses within a defined radius
  • Identify skills and services available within the community before hiring outside
  • Use existing communication channels — email lists, church bulletins, neighborhood apps — to promote local businesses
Step-by-Step Action Guide
  1. Survey community members for businesses and professional services
  2. Build a simple directory (Google Sheets is sufficient to start)
  3. Share the directory widely and update it regularly
  4. Host one quarterly "Shop Local" event to connect residents and businesses
  5. Connect business owners with mentors and resources at local SBDCs
  6. Identify gaps in local services that community businesses could fill
Resources
  • ByBlack (US Black Chambers)Directory and certification for Black-owned businesses nationwide.
  • BuyBlack.orgMarketplace and directory supporting Black-owned businesses.
  • SBA Local Assistance FinderLocate your nearest Small Business Development Center for free business counseling. (Verify current availability at sba.gov.)
Measure Progress
  • Number of businesses listed in the directory
  • Attendance at local business events
  • Number of referrals made between community members and local businesses
  • New businesses started or expanded with community support
Section B / Economic Power

Wealth Building & Financial Literacy

Closing wealth gaps requires sustained community education, access to trusted financial professionals, and connections to financial institutions that serve Black communities. This section focuses on what community organizations can realistically build and facilitate.

Disclaimer: This guide provides general educational information, not financial advice. Residents seeking personalized guidance on investments, taxes, insurance, or estate planning should consult a licensed financial professional. Verify the credentials of any advisor before sharing personal financial information.

What We Can Build Together
  • A financial literacy workshop series covering budgeting, credit, savings, and insurance
  • A trusted referral network of Black financial advisors, CPAs, and insurance professionals
  • A community investment club for shared learning (consult legal counsel before pooling funds)
  • Connections to CDFIs (Community Development Financial Institutions) for affordable loans
  • An estate planning awareness campaign encouraging wills, beneficiary designations, and life insurance
Start Small

A one-hour financial literacy workshop at a church, library, or community center costs almost nothing if facilitated by a volunteer professional. Start with one topic — credit scores, emergency savings, or life insurance basics — before expanding to a full series.

Partner with a local CDFI or credit union to host, so participants can open accounts or apply for services the same day.

Resources
  • Opportunity Finance Network (CDFIs)Find a Community Development Financial Institution offering affordable lending in your area.
  • Consumer Financial Protection BureauFree tools, guides, and resources for financial education.
  • Giving GapResearch and resources on philanthropy and wealth-building in Black communities.
Measure Progress
  • Number of residents who attended financial education events
  • Number of referrals to trusted financial professionals
  • Number of residents who opened savings accounts, started emergency funds, or obtained life insurance
  • Community members who completed wills or estate planning documents

Note: financial outcomes are private. Track participation and self-reported changes, not personal account details.

Section C / Essential Needs

Food Security & Community Agriculture

Food insecurity affects millions of Americans and is disproportionately experienced in Black communities. Community-based food initiatives — from gardens to co-ops to distribution networks — can increase access, build relationships, and strengthen local food systems.

What We Can Build Together
  • A community garden on available land (church property, school grounds, vacant lots)
  • A community food pantry or mutual aid food distribution network
  • A food cooperative with member purchasing power
  • A neighborhood farmers market connecting local growers to residents
  • A nutrition and cooking education program for families
Start With What We Have
  • Survey your congregation or neighborhood for gardeners, farmers, and food professionals
  • Identify available outdoor space: church lots, school grounds, backyards willing to share
  • Connect with your county extension service for free soil testing and gardening assistance
  • Identify existing food pantries and mutual aid networks before starting a new one
  • Connect with local restaurants, grocery stores, or food distributors about surplus food donation
Start Small: A 4-Bed Community Garden

A four raised-bed garden on a 10x20 foot area at a church or school can feed several families and build community — without a formal nonprofit or significant funding.

  • Request permission for a small plot from a property owner willing to partner
  • Form a five-person care team with clear tasks: watering, weeding, harvesting, communications
  • Request seed donations from local garden centers or apply to a seed library
  • Share the harvest transparently with participants and with neighbors in need
  • Document what you grew, who was fed, and what you learned
Resources
  • Black Farmers IndexDirectory and resources supporting Black farmers across the United States.
  • Black Farmer FundFunding and technical support for Black farmers in the Northeast and beyond.
  • Farmers' AlmanacPlanting guides, seasonal schedules, and gardening resources for any region.
  • Feeding America Food Bank LocatorFind your nearest food bank for partnership and food sourcing. (Verify at feedingamerica.org.)
Measure Progress
  • Pounds of food produced or distributed
  • Number of households served
  • Volunteer hours contributed
  • Number of community members who gained gardening or food preservation skills
  • Cost savings compared to purchasing equivalent produce
Build Partnerships
  • Local schools — school gardens, nutrition education, student involvement
  • County Cooperative Extension office — free technical assistance, soil testing, master gardener volunteers
  • Local hospitals and health departments — healthy food access and nutrition programs
  • Regional food banks — surplus food, storage equipment, and distribution support
  • Black farmers and urban agriculture programs in your region

Housing Stability & Access

Stable housing is foundational to family well-being, educational outcomes, and community health. Community organizations can support housing stability by connecting residents with information, legal resources, and trusted housing counselors.

What We Can Build Together
  • A housing resource guide for your area: assistance programs, legal aid, HUD counselors
  • A tenant rights education workshop with a legal aid attorney
  • A home repair assistance network connecting skilled volunteers to elderly homeowners
  • An emergency rental assistance referral system for residents facing eviction
  • A homeownership readiness cohort for renters who want to buy
Resources
  • HUD-Approved Housing CounselorsFree or low-cost counseling for homebuyers, renters, and people facing foreclosure.
  • LawHelp.orgFind free and low-cost legal aid organizations in your state for housing issues.
  • National Low Income Housing CoalitionPolicy research and advocacy on affordable housing. Find your state's resources.

  • Number of residents connected to housing assistance resources

Start Small

Host one housing resource fair at a church or community center with three to five local organizations: a HUD-approved housing counselor, a legal aid attorney, a CDFI, and a local housing authority representative.

This costs little but connects residents with trusted professionals they might not know about.

Section C / Essential Needs

Mental Health & Emotional Well-Being

Mental health stigma remains a significant barrier in many Black communities. Community organizations can help by normalizing mental health conversations, building trusted referral networks, and creating spaces where people feel safe asking for support.

Crisis Resources: If someone is in immediate danger, call 911. For mental health crises, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline is available 24/7 by calling or texting 988. The Crisis Text Line is available by texting HOME to 741741.

What We Can Build Together
  • A trusted referral list of Black therapists, counselors, and psychiatrists in your area
  • A mental health awareness workshop series at a church, school, or community center
  • A peer support group facilitated by a trained community member
  • A mental health first aid training for community leaders and volunteers
  • Destigmatization campaigns using storytelling, social media, and community events
Resources
  • Therapy for Black PeopleDirectory of Black therapists and mental health providers. (Verify current availability.)
  • Mental Health First Aid USATraining programs that teach community members to recognize and respond to mental health crises.
  • 988 Suicide & Crisis LifelineFree, confidential crisis support available 24/7 by calling or texting 988.
  • National Alliance on Mental Illness (NAMI)Education, support groups, and advocacy resources for individuals and families.
Cultural Considerations
  • Partner with faith leaders to address mental health from a spiritual and holistic perspective, not in opposition to it
  • Avoid language that pathologizes or shames — focus on wellness, strength, and support
  • Acknowledge that systemic racism, poverty, and trauma are valid stressors, not personal failures
  • Prioritize Black therapists for referrals when possible — cultural competence matters
  • Create safe, judgment-free spaces where people can share without risk of gossip or social consequences
Start Small

A one-hour mental health awareness conversation at a regular church or community meeting — with a trained facilitator, no clinical content, just honest discussion — can be transformative and costs nothing.

Start by normalizing the conversation. Programs can follow once trust is established.

Section C / Essential Needs

Emergency Preparedness & Mutual Aid

Natural disasters, power outages, and public health emergencies disproportionately affect communities with fewer institutional resources. Community-level preparedness — built on existing relationships — saves lives and accelerates recovery.

What We Can Build Together
  • A neighborhood emergency contact network with current phone numbers and addresses
  • A mutual aid fund for immediate disaster response — even $50 per household builds meaningful reserves
  • An emergency supply depot at a church or community center with water, first aid, and shelf-stable food
  • A vulnerability map identifying elderly, disabled, and medically dependent neighbors who need extra support
  • A community emergency response team (CERT) trained through your local fire department
Resources
  • Ready.govFederal emergency preparedness resources including family and community planning guides.
  • FEMA Community Emergency Response Teams (CERT)Free training for community members in basic disaster response skills.
  • Mutual Aid HubResources for starting and running community mutual aid networks.
Start Small: Neighbor Check-In System

Identify five neighbors on your street who are willing to check on each other during an emergency. Exchange phone numbers. Identify who has a car, who has medical needs, who lives alone.

This requires no funding, no organization, and no permit. It requires only a conversation.

Community Action Checklist
Create a household emergency plan with evacuation routes and contact numbers
Store at least 72 hours of water and food for each household member
Know the locations of your nearest emergency shelter and hospital
Identify elderly or disabled neighbors who may need assistance
Connect with a neighborhood mutual aid or emergency contact group
Complete a CERT or first aid/CPR training
Section C / Essential Needs

Community Safety

Community safety is a collective responsibility that goes beyond policing. Neighborhood relationships, trusted institutions, economic opportunity, youth engagement, and mental health resources all contribute to safe communities. This section focuses on what residents and organizations can build together.

What We Can Build Together
  • A neighborhood watch or block association that builds relationships and shares information
  • A violence interruption program in partnership with trained community mediators
  • Safe spaces and mentorship programs for young people at highest risk
  • Community dialogue events to address conflicts before they escalate
  • Coordination with local police on community concerns — while maintaining independent community accountability
Prevention Over Response

Research consistently shows that investments in education, economic opportunity, mental health, housing stability, and youth programs reduce community violence more sustainably than enforcement-only approaches.

Community safety work is most effective when it addresses root causes alongside immediate concerns. A safe community is one where people know each other, where young people have opportunities, and where those in crisis can get help.

Resources
  • Cure Violence GlobalEvidence-based violence interruption programs and training resources. (Verify current programs.)
  • Community Justice ExchangeResources on community-based safety approaches and alternatives to incarceration.
Start Small: Know Your Neighbors

A block party, a shared meal, or a neighborhood walk costs almost nothing and accomplishes one of the most evidence-based safety interventions: neighbors knowing each other by name.

Communities where neighbors have strong social connections report greater feelings of safety and faster response to concerns.

Digital access and literacy are now fundamental to economic participation, educational success, and civic engagement. AI and Your Community

Artificial intelligence tools are increasingly present in hiring, lending, healthcare, criminal justice, and housing — areas that directly affect Black communities. Community conversations about AI do not require technical expertise. They require asking questions: Who built this system? Who benefits? What happens when it makes a mistake?

Section D / Education & Future

Nursing & Healthcare Careers

Nursing and allied health professions offer stable careers with strong compensation, geographic flexibility, and community impact. This section outlines pathways from entry-level positions to advanced practice roles, and how communities can support healthcare career development.

Nursing offers one of the most direct pathways to middle-class and professional-class income available through community college-accessible credentials. An LPN can earn a livable wage with one year of training. An RN-to-BSN bridge can be completed while working. An NP or CRNA represents significant earning power reachable through deliberate steps.

Nursing Career Pathways
CredentialTrainingRole
CNA4–12 weeksCertified Nursing Assistant — entry-level patient care

  • Host a healthcare careers night with current nursing students and professionals

  • Create a study group or support network for nursing students in your community
  • Share information about hospital tuition reimbursement programs
  • Recognize and celebrate community members who complete healthcare credentials

Civic Participation & Advocacy

Civic engagement — voting, attending public meetings, contacting elected officials, and running for office — is how communities shape the policies and investments that affect their daily lives. These are rights and responsibilities, not just options.

What We Can Build Together
  • A nonpartisan voter registration drive before every election
  • A candidate forum where community members can ask questions of local candidates
  • A civic education series on how local government works: city council, school board, county commission
  • A community advocacy team that tracks legislation and attends public hearings on key issues
  • Voter protection efforts including poll monitoring and ride-to-polls programs
Local Government Matters Most

Local elections — school board, city council, sheriff, district attorney, county commission — have the most direct impact on daily community life and are often decided by very small margins. Yet local elections typically have the lowest Black voter turnout.

Understanding who makes decisions about schools, policing, zoning, parks, and public health in your area is the starting point for effective advocacy.

Resources
  • Vote.orgVoter registration, absentee ballot requests, and polling place lookup for all 50 states.

  • Word in BlackNews and analysis from Black media organizations covering civic and policy issues.
Start Small: Know Your Officials

Identify the name, contact information, and term expiration for your city council member, school board member, and state legislative representative. Share this information in your next community meeting or church bulletin.

Most community members do not know who represents them locally. That information deficit is where civic disengagement begins.

Section E / Family & Wellness

Essential Protections: Insurance & Financial Safety Nets

Life insurance, health insurance, disability coverage, and estate planning are essential protections that many families lack. Community organizations can help by providing education and connecting residents with trusted professionals — not by selling products.

Disclaimer: This section is for educational purposes only. It is not financial or insurance advice. Residents should consult a licensed insurance professional and/or financial advisor for personalized guidance. Be cautious of anyone who profits from the products they recommend without a fiduciary duty to you.

What We Can Educate About
  • Life insurance: Term vs. whole life; the importance of having coverage before it is needed
  • Health insurance: ACA marketplace enrollment, Medicaid eligibility, CHIP for children
  • Disability insurance: Most families are one serious illness away from financial crisis without it
  • Wills and beneficiary designations: Assets without proper designations may not go to intended heirs
  • Advance directives: Healthcare power of attorney and living wills protect families during medical crises
Start Small: A Wills Workshop

A one-time workshop at a church or community center with a licensed estate planning attorney costs little to the attendees (many attorneys provide these pro bono for community events) and can help dozens of families put basic protections in place.

Focus on the basics that most people lack: a simple will, beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and insurance policies, and a healthcare power of attorney.

Section F / Institutions & Partners

Faith Communities & Institutional Partners

Faith communities have historically been anchors of Black community life — and they are one of many sources of strength. The barber shop, the beauty salon, the corner store, the family reunion, the neighborhood cookout: these spaces have always been where community is built. Institutions are partners in that work, not the origin of it.

What Faith Communities Can Contribute
  • Meeting space for community gatherings, workshops, and programs
  • Communication networks reaching members and the wider neighborhood
  • Volunteer mobilization capacity
  • Credibility and trust that new organizations often lack
  • Financial resources through tithes, offerings, and institutional giving
  • Existing relationships with vulnerable community members
Healthy Partnership Boundaries
  • Faith communities should not impose theological requirements on secular community programs they host
  • Community programs should not assume all community members are or want to be church members
  • Financial contributions and facility use should be documented in a simple written agreement
  • Decision-making authority over community programs should be shared with the broader community, not only the host institution's leadership
  • Multiple faith communities should be invited to participate — not just the largest or most prominent
Other Institutional Partners
  • HBCUs and community colleges: Research capacity, student volunteers, workforce pipelines
  • Hospitals and health systems: Community benefit requirements, health education, clinical connections
  • School districts: Facilities, family engagement, youth pipelines
  • CDFIs and credit unions: Affordable capital, financial education, business development
  • Foundations and corporate partners: Funding, in-kind support, pro bono services
Resources
  • Giving GapResearch and resources on giving, philanthropy, and resource development in Black communities.
  • National Council of NonprofitsManagement resources, policy updates, and nonprofit best practices.
Section F / Institutions & Partners

Policy Engagement & Long-Term Sustainability

Community organizations that engage in policy advocacy can influence the decisions that shape their neighborhoods. This requires building relationships with public officials, tracking relevant legislation, and communicating community needs credibly and consistently.

What We Can Build Together
  • A policy tracking system monitoring local and state legislation affecting the community
  • Regular relationships with city council members, school board members, and other local officials
  • Testimony at public hearings on issues affecting the community
  • A community data report documenting needs, assets, and outcomes to inform public investment
  • Participation in city and county budget processes where community priorities can be included
Organizational Sustainability
  • Diversify funding: grants, individual donors, earned income, in-kind support — no single source should exceed 30-40% of revenue
  • Build a reserve fund covering three to six months of operating expenses
  • Document programs, contacts, and processes so knowledge is not lost when leaders change
  • Conduct an annual review of programs: which are working, which should change, which should stop
  • Develop board members and volunteers who can lead financially and legally
