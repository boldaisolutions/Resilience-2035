The Foundation

The strategy is not complicated. Build institutions that generate wealth, protect health, educate children, and circulate resources inside the community. The goal is not separation. The goal is self-sufficiency: the ability to meet your own needs whether or not external systems serve you fairly.

This manual is organized around five pillars. Each section of this site maps to one or more of these pillars and provides the tools to act on it today.

Pillar 01 / Own More

Economic Development

Black Americans generate over $1.6 trillion in annual consumer spending. Less than 2 cents of every dollar circulates within the Black community before leaving. Economic resilience requires redirecting that spending into Black-owned enterprises, keeping dollars circulating locally, and building the commercial infrastructure to sustain it.

The Problem

The Black dollar circulates in the Black community for approximately 6 hours before leaving, compared to 20 days in white communities, 28 days in Jewish communities, and 19 days in Asian communities (Watkins, 2016). This is not a cultural failure. It is a structural one: Black communities lack sufficient Black-owned grocery stores, banks, pharmacies, and professional services to retain spending locally.

Black business ownership rates remain significantly lower than ownership rates in other demographic groups, largely due to unequal access to startup capital, business credit, and professional networks (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, 2021).

Individual Actions
  • Audit your monthly spending and identify three to five categories where a Black-owned alternative exists
  • Download the Official Black Wall Street or EatOkra app to locate Black-owned businesses
  • Redirect at least 25% of your discretionary spending to Black-owned businesses over six months
  • Leave public reviews for Black-owned businesses you patronize
  • If you own a business, list it on Black-owned business directories
  • Recruit two people in your network to do the same
Family Actions
  • Create a family spending plan that prioritizes Black-owned grocery, insurance, and financial services
  • Teach children to research businesses before spending
  • Start or join a family investment club (minimum $25/month per member)
  • Discuss Black economic history: Greenwood District, Black Wall Street, cooperative economics
  • Support family members who start businesses with time, skills, or referrals
Church and Nonprofit Actions
  • Create a Black business directory for your congregation
  • Host an annual Black Business Expo on church grounds
  • Establish a small business incubator in unused church space
  • Redirect church vendor contracts to Black-owned suppliers where possible
  • Partner with a CDFI to offer small business loans to congregation members
Community Organization Actions
  • Establish a local Black Chamber of Commerce chapter
  • Launch a cooperative business directory with verified listings
  • Create a mentorship matching program pairing established and emerging entrepreneurs
  • Advocate for municipal set-aside contracts for Black-owned businesses
  • Organize a community buying club for bulk purchasing from Black-owned suppliers
Priority Sectors to Build

  • Trucking and logistics

  • Insurance agencies

Pillar 02 / Grow More

Food Security

Approximately 21.2% of Black Americans experience food insecurity, nearly twice the national average. Food security is not simply a hunger issue. It is a health equity issue, an economic issue, and a sovereignty issue. Communities that grow their own food are harder to destabilize.

The Problem

Black neighborhoods are disproportionately located in food deserts, areas where residents are more than one mile from a supermarket in urban areas or more than ten miles in rural areas (USDA, 2021). Food insecurity is compounded by diet-related chronic disease rates: Black Americans have significantly higher rates of hypertension, diabetes, and obesity, conditions directly linked to poor food access (CDC, 2023).

Local food production, cooperative buying, and community agriculture are proven interventions that build food sovereignty regardless of external market conditions.

Individual Actions
  • Plant a container or backyard garden with high-yield vegetables (collards, tomatoes, beans, herbs)
  • Learn preservation techniques: canning, freezing, pickling, dehydrating
  • Join or start a Community Supported Agriculture (CSA) share with a Black-owned farm
  • Purchase from Black farmers markets when available
  • Learn basic nutrition tied to preventing the chronic diseases most affecting the community
Family Actions
  • Allocate 10% of your food budget to a bulk buying cooperative
  • Teach children to grow at least one food crop per season
  • Build a 30-day pantry reserve of staple foods
  • Connect with your county extension office for free gardening and canning resources
  • Join a local food cooperative as a member-owner
Church and Nonprofit Actions
  • Convert unused church land into a community garden or church farm
  • Partner with urban agriculture organizations for technical assistance
  • Host monthly food distribution as a congregation anchor institution
  • Establish a food pantry stocked with healthy, culturally relevant foods
  • Launch a cooking and nutrition education series for youth and seniors
Community Organization Actions
  • Establish a food cooperative with member ownership structure
  • Launch a mobile grocery truck in underserved neighborhoods
  • Create a regional network connecting Black-owned farms to urban consumers
  • Develop hydroponics or aquaponics systems in available warehouse space
  • Organize a community seed library and preservation program
Startup Costs

Community garden (10 plots): $2,000 to $8,000 (land prep, fencing, tools, water)

Food cooperative (startup): $50,000 to $200,000

Pillar 04 / Protect More

Healthcare

Black Americans face a life expectancy gap of approximately 5 years compared to white Americans, a gap driven primarily by preventable chronic disease, maternal mortality, and inequitable access to quality care. Building Black-controlled healthcare infrastructure is a matter of survival.

The Problem

Black women are 2.6 times more likely to die from pregnancy-related complications than white women (CDC, 2023). Black Americans have the highest rates of hypertension of any racial or ethnic group. Research consistently shows that Black patients receive better care, experience less dismissal of symptoms, and have better outcomes when treated by Black physicians (Greenwood et al., 2020).

The solution is dual: advocate for equity within existing systems while building parallel Black-controlled healthcare networks that can serve the community regardless of policy changes.

Individual Actions
  • Identify a Black primary care physician, dentist, and mental health provider
  • Complete annual wellness visits even when healthy
  • Learn your family's complete medical history, especially cardiovascular and diabetes
  • Understand your health insurance benefits fully
  • Track your blood pressure, blood glucose, and weight monthly
  • Eliminate or reduce tobacco use
Family Actions
  • Schedule annual wellness visits for every family member
  • Create a family health history document and share it with your physician
  • Discuss mental health openly, reducing stigma in your household
  • Ensure children have a pediatrician and are current on vaccinations
  • Build a three-month supply of essential prescription medications
Church and Nonprofit Actions
  • Host free quarterly health screening events (blood pressure, glucose, cholesterol)
  • Partner with a Black medical school or teaching hospital for community clinics
  • Establish a church health ministry led by healthcare professionals in the congregation
  • Train congregation members in Mental Health First Aid
  • Create a referral network of Black healthcare providers
Grants and Funding

Pillar 03 / Teach More

Education

Education is the single highest-return investment a person can make, but the pathway matters. This section covers every legitimate route: four-year college, community college, certifications, trade schools, apprenticeships, and AI-era digital credentials. The goal is maximizing earning potential and career options, not prestige for its own sake.

The Problem

The Black-white college enrollment gap has narrowed but the completion gap remains significant: Black students are less likely to complete a four-year degree within six years, largely due to financial barriers and lack of institutional support (NCES, 2022). At the same time, vocational and technical credentials in high-demand fields (cybersecurity, cloud computing, electrical, HVAC) are producing median wages of $55,000 to $95,000 without four-year degree requirements.

The right question is not "college or trades" but "which pathway generates the best return relative to cost, timeline, and your goals?"

Individual Actions
  • Research demand data before choosing a major or certification (Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook)
  • Complete FAFSA every year by October 1 to maximize aid eligibility
  • Consider community college for the first two years (same degree, 60-70% lower cost)
  • Pursue employer tuition assistance before taking on personal debt
  • Earn Google, AWS, Microsoft, or Cisco certifications alongside or instead of a degree in technology fields

  • CompTIA Security+: $370 exam, cybersecurity entry, $65K-$90K

  • Microsoft Azure Fundamentals: $165 exam, IT administration
  • Cisco CCNA: networking and infrastructure, $65K-$85K

Pillar 04 / Protect More

Political Engagement

Most decisions affecting day-to-day life happen at the local and state level: school boards, zoning boards, city councils, county commissions, and state legislatures. Federal elections matter, but local elections determine your schools, your policing, your zoning, and your economic development priorities.

The Problem

Black voter turnout in local elections averages between 15% and 30%, compared to 50-65% in presidential elections (CIRCLE, 2022). Yet city councils and school boards make the decisions most directly affecting families. When Black communities do not vote locally, others make decisions about Black neighborhoods, schools, police departments, and economic development zones.

Individual Actions
  • Register to vote and verify your registration before every election cycle
  • Vote in every election: presidential, state, county, municipal, and school board
  • Attend at least two city council meetings per year
  • Submit public comments on zoning, policing, and budget decisions
  • Research candidates' positions on housing, education, and policing before voting
Community Organization Actions
  • Conduct year-round voter registration, not just in election years
  • Train poll workers and election judges from the community
  • Host candidate forums on issues specific to the Black community
  • Develop a nonpartisan voter guide covering local races
  • Build a civic education program for high school students
Key Local Positions to Target
  • School board members (control curriculum, budgets, discipline policies)
  • City council and county commission
  • District attorney and county sheriff
  • State legislature
  • Planning and zoning commissions
  • Water and utility boards
National Organizations

Pillar 01 / Own More

Financial Infrastructure

Financial literacy is important, but it is not sufficient. You cannot budget your way out of poverty. The real levers of financial progress are income growth, career development, asset ownership, and entrepreneurship. This section focuses on building the infrastructure for lasting wealth, not just managing what is already there.

The Problem

The racial wealth gap is structural. The median Black family holds approximately $24,100 in wealth compared to $188,200 for the median white family (Federal Reserve, 2023). This gap is not explained by spending habits or financial literacy. It is the result of centuries of exclusion from wealth-building systems: redlining, discriminatory lending, exclusion from GI Bill benefits, and ongoing wage gaps.

Financial literacy education alone cannot overcome low wages, medical debt, housing costs, lack of startup capital, or structural barriers. The strategy must focus on income growth, asset ownership, and building the cooperative financial institutions that can serve the community when traditional banks will not.

Individual Actions
  • Open accounts at a Black-owned bank or credit union
  • Establish a six-month emergency fund before aggressive investing
  • Contribute to your employer's 401(k) at least to the full employer match
  • Open a Roth IRA and contribute annually
  • Review your credit report at annualcreditreport.com (free weekly)
  • Pursue income growth through negotiation, promotion, second income streams, or entrepreneurship

  • OneUnited Bank: oneunited.com
  • Citizens Trust Bank: citizenstrust.com
  • Broadway Federal Bank: broadwayfederalbank.com

  • Partner with a CDFI to create a micro-lending fund
  • Create a financial coaching program for community members
  • Advocate for a community credit union
  • Launch an IDA (Individual Development Account) matched savings program

Pillar 01 / Own More

Wealth Building

Wealth is not income. Wealth is what you own minus what you owe. Building wealth requires owning assets that appreciate in value, generate income, and can be transferred to the next generation. Every dollar spent on consumption is gone. Every dollar invested in an asset can grow.

The Problem

The racial wealth gap persists across income levels. A Black family earning $80,000 per year has significantly less wealth than a white family earning the same income, largely because of differences in homeownership rates, business ownership, and inheritance (Darity & Mullen, 2020). Closing the gap requires not just earning more but owning more.

Investment Vehicles
  • Index funds: Low-cost, diversified stock market exposure. Vanguard, Fidelity, Schwab.
  • ETFs: Similar to index funds, trade like stocks, very low fees.
  • Roth IRA: Tax-free growth. Contribute up to $7,000/year (2024). Open at any brokerage.
  • 401(k) / 403(b): Pre-tax retirement accounts. Always contribute to full employer match.
  • 529 Plan: Tax-advantaged college savings for children.
  • Investment clubs: Pool capital with others to invest collectively.
Asset Ownership Beyond Stocks
  • Homeownership: Primary path to wealth for most families. 30-year fixed mortgage builds equity.
  • Rental property: Income-generating real estate. Start with a duplex, live in one unit.
  • Commercial real estate: Higher barrier to entry, higher returns. REITs allow smaller investments.
  • Farmland: Appreciating asset, lease income. AcreTrader.com for fractional farmland investment.
  • Storage units and laundromats: Recession-resistant cash-flow businesses.
  • Franchises: Proven business model with brand recognition.
Intellectual Property
  • Patents: Protect inventions; can be licensed for royalties
  • Trademarks: Protect brand names and logos
  • Copyrights: Protect music, books, art, software
  • USPTO: uspto.gov for patent and trademark filings
  • IP creates passive income streams that compound over time
Generational Wealth Transfer
  • Create a will, trust, and beneficiary designations on all accounts
  • Establish a living trust to avoid probate costs (especially important for property)
  • Purchase life insurance to protect family wealth in the event of early death
  • Teach children about investing, compound interest, and asset ownership
  • Set up custodial brokerage accounts for minor children

  2. Collins, C., Asante-Muhammad, D., Hoxie, J., & Nieves, E. (2019). Dreams deferred: How enriching the 1% widens the racial wealth divide. Institute for Policy Studies.

Pillar 03 / Teach More

Technology & AI

AI and automation are reshaping every industry. Communities that develop technology skills will create the next generation of entrepreneurs, consultants, and high-income earners. Communities that do not will be left performing tasks that AI will soon automate. The time to build these skills is now.

The Problem

Black workers are underrepresented in technology occupations relative to the overall workforce. Only 7% of computing and mathematical occupations are held by Black workers (BLS, 2023). This gap means Black communities will disproportionately bear the negative effects of AI-driven job displacement while being underrepresented among those who build and profit from AI systems.

Pillar 01 / Own More

Land & Property

Land is the foundation of generational wealth. It cannot be reproduced, it appreciates over time, and it can generate income through farming, rental, timber, and development. Black land ownership peaked at approximately 15 million acres in 1910. Today it stands at approximately 2.5 million acres. Reclaiming land ownership is a generational priority.

The Problem

The loss of Black-owned land between 1910 and 2020 represents one of the largest wealth transfers in American history, driven by heir property vulnerability, tax sales, discriminatory USDA lending, and outright fraud and violence (Darity & Mullen, 2020). Heir property, land passed down without formal estate planning, affects an estimated 40% of Black landowners in the South and makes property vulnerable to partition sales.

Individual Actions
  • Create a will and living trust now, even if you rent
  • Clear heir property titles: contact Land Loss Prevention Project or Heirs' Property Law Center
  • Research your county's tax auction schedule for below-market land opportunities
  • Study Community Land Trust (CLT) models for affordable homeownership pathways
  • Consider purchasing raw land in growing suburban corridors before prices increase
Community Organization Actions
  • Establish a Community Land Trust to hold land in perpetual community ownership
  • Partner with a Land Bank to acquire and rehabilitate vacant properties
  • Create an estate planning clinic to clear heir property titles
  • Develop affordable housing on community-owned land
  • Advocate for USDA Heirs' Property Relending Program access

The economy that today's youth will enter is fundamentally different from the one their parents navigated. Jobs that paid well for decades will be automated.   • Basic bookkeeping and accounting clerks (automated by accounting software)

Essential Protections

There are foundational documents, accounts, and protections every Black American family needs. These are not luxuries. They are tools of security, mobility, and self-determination. This section covers the documents and protections your household should have in place within the next 12 months.

Passports

A U.S. passport is the most powerful identity document you can carry. It is accepted as ID in all 50 states, allows international travel, and can be used to establish identity in any jurisdiction. Apply at travel.state.gov.

Cost: $130 for adults, $100 for children under 16

Time: Apply 3-6 months before planned travel; routine processing takes 6-8 weeks

Priority: Every adult and child should have a valid passport

  • Passport acceptance facilities: post offices, libraries, courthouses
  • Reduced-fee passports: available at some Legal Aid offices for low-income applicants
  • State Department fee waiver: for those demonstrating financial need
Life Insurance

Life insurance replaces income and covers final expenses. Without it, a family can lose their home, savings, and stability when a breadwinner dies. The goal is 10-12 times your annual income in coverage.

  • Term life insurance: Lowest cost, covers a specific period (20-30 years). Best for most families.
  • A healthy 35-year-old can get $500,000 of 20-year term coverage for $25-$35/month
  • Compare quotes: policygenius.com, term4sale.com
  • Black-owned insurance companies: National Business League directory
  • Purchase enough to pay off your mortgage, fund your children's education, and replace your income for 10 years
Renter's Insurance

If you rent, your landlord's insurance does not cover your belongings. A single fire, flood, or theft can cost tens of thousands of dollars. Renter's insurance costs $15-$30 per month.

  • Compare quotes: lemonade.com, progressive.com, statefarm.com
  • Coverage: personal property, liability, temporary housing if displaced
  • Take a video inventory of your belongings for insurance documentation
  • Most policies cover theft outside your home (a stolen laptop from a coffee shop)
College Scholarships

Scholarship applications take time but can eliminate tens of thousands of dollars in debt. Most applicants fail not because of competition but because they do not apply.

  • Start here: Scholarships.com, fastweb.com, bold.org
  • UNCF: uncf.org (hundreds of scholarships for Black students)
  • Thurgood Marshall: tmcf.org
  • Ron Brown Scholar Program: ronbrown.org
  • HBCU institutional scholarships: contact admissions offices directly
  • Local scholarships: often the least competitive; check your community foundation
  • Apply to at least 20 scholarships per semester
Additional Protections
  • Will and advance directive: Free at many legal aid offices, freewill.com
  • Health insurance: Open enrollment at healthcare.gov; check Medicaid eligibility
  • Disability insurance: Protects income if you cannot work due to injury or illness
  • Homeowner's insurance: Required by mortgage lenders; compare quotes annually
  • Umbrella policy: Inexpensive additional liability protection for property owners
  • Identity theft protection: Credit freeze at all three bureaus (free): equifax.com, experian.com, transunion.com
Startup Costs

Passport: $130 per adult

Term life insurance: $25-$60/month depending on age and health

Renter's insurance: $15-$30/month

Basic will: Free at legal aid or $99 at freewill.com

Total monthly commitment for a family: approximately $60-$120 covers all insurance protections

Health & Wellness

Health is wealth. Medical debt is the leading cause of personal bankruptcy in the United States, and Black Americans experience disproportionate rates of chronic disease that are largely preventable. This section focuses on prevention, self-management, and building the habits that protect long-term earning capacity and quality of life.

Individual Actions
  • Complete an annual physical and comprehensive lab work every year
  • Know your numbers: blood pressure (under 120/80), fasting glucose (under 100), cholesterol (total under 200)
  • Aim for 150 minutes of moderate exercise per week (CDC minimum)
  • Maintain a healthy weight: BMI 18.5-24.9, but waist circumference is also a key indicator
  • Eliminate tobacco in all forms
  • Limit alcohol to moderate consumption or eliminate it
  • Prioritize 7-9 hours of sleep per night
  • Manage chronic stress through exercise, community, spiritual practice, and therapy when needed
Chronic Disease Prevention

Black Americans have the highest rates of hypertension of any demographic group. Uncontrolled hypertension leads to stroke, kidney failure, and heart attack. It is called "the silent killer" because it produces no symptoms until organ damage occurs.

  • Check blood pressure monthly; home monitors cost $25-$50
  • Reduce sodium intake to under 2,300mg/day
  • DASH diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension): proven to lower blood pressure
  • Diabetes prevention: maintain healthy weight, limit refined carbohydrates and sugar
  • Get screened for diabetes if overweight or with family history
Church Wellness Ministry
  • Establish a health ministry led by nurses, physicians, or health educators in the congregation
  • Host quarterly health screenings (blood pressure, glucose, cholesterol)
  • Launch a walking club or fitness group that meets weekly
  • Partner with a local YMCA for discounted membership for congregation members
  • Offer cooking classes focused on heart-healthy, culturally relevant recipes
  • Host mental health workshops to reduce stigma and connect members with providers
Maternal Health

Black women are 2.6 times more likely to die from pregnancy-related causes than white women. This disparity is not explained by income or education level. It is driven by systemic under-treatment, dismissal of symptoms by medical providers, and lack of culturally competent care.

  • Seek out Black OB/GYN providers where possible
  • Consider hiring a Black doula for labor and postpartum support
  • Know your rights to advocate for yourself in a hospital setting
  • Black Mamas Matter Alliance: blackmamasmatter.org
  • Postpartum Support International: postpartum.net

Emergency Preparedness

Hurricane Katrina, the 2021 Texas winter storm, and COVID-19 all demonstrated that Black communities are disproportionately harmed when emergencies occur and that government response is not always immediate. Prepared communities survive. Unprepared ones depend on systems that may not prioritize them.

Household Preparedness

FEMA recommends a minimum 72-hour supply of essentials. A two-week supply is more appropriate for realistic emergencies.

  • Water: 1 gallon per person per day for 14 days
  • Food: 14-day supply of non-perishable staples (rice, beans, canned goods, oats)
  • Medications: 30-90 day supply of all critical prescriptions
  • First aid kit: Fully stocked, including tourniquets and wound care
  • Documents: Physical copies of passports, birth certificates, insurance cards, wills
  • Cash: ATMs go offline during power outages; keep $200-$500 in small bills
  • Backup power: Portable generator or solar charger
  • Communication plan: Designated out-of-state contact, family meeting point
Community Preparedness
  • Map every vulnerable person (elderly, disabled, infants) within two blocks
  • Establish a phone tree or group chat with 20-30 neighbors
  • Identify neighbors who have generators, medical training, or vehicles
  • Stock a community emergency supply cache at the church or community center
  • Train 10+ community members in CPR/AED and basic first aid through Red Cross
  • Create a neighborhood mutual aid plan for extended emergencies
Financial Emergency Preparedness
  • Six-month emergency fund in a high-yield savings account
  • Adequate insurance coverage before emergencies occur
  • Know FEMA disaster assistance application process: disasterassistance.gov
  • Know your rights as a renter or homeowner during declared disasters
  • Community emergency loan fund: pre-established before it is needed
Resources
  • FEMA Ready: ready.gov
  • American Red Cross: redcross.org (CPR/first aid training)
  • Community Emergency Response Team (CERT): citizencorps.fema.gov
  • FEMA Individual Assistance: disasterassistance.gov
  • Mutual Aid Hub: mutualaidhub.org

Mental Health

Mental health is health. The trauma of racism, economic stress, community violence, and generational loss takes a measurable physiological toll. Black communities have historically under-utilized mental health services due to stigma, cost, and lack of culturally competent providers. Building mental health infrastructure is not optional. It is foundational to every other goal in this manual.

Critical Clarification on Mental Health First Aid: Mental Health First Aid (MHFA) participants are NOT therapists. They cannot diagnose mental health conditions. They cannot provide counseling. They cannot replace licensed mental health professionals. Their role is to: identify warning signs, provide initial non-clinical support, reduce stigma in their community, and connect individuals with licensed mental health providers or emergency services when needed. MHFA training is a first step, not a solution.

  • Crisis support: 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988)
  • Crisis Text Line: text HOME to 741741
  • Practice reducing mental health stigma in your own conversations

Pillar 01 / Own More

Housing

Homeownership remains the primary vehicle for wealth accumulation for middle-class Americans. The Black homeownership rate (43%) is approximately 29 percentage points below the white homeownership rate, a gap that has widened since the 1960s despite the Fair Housing Act. Closing this gap through homeownership, community development, and anti-displacement strategies is a generational priority.

The Problem

Redlining, the systematic denial of FHA loans and insurance to Black neighborhoods from the 1930s through the 1960s, prevented an entire generation from accessing the wealth-building tool that white families used to build the American middle class. The geographic and economic effects of redlining are still measurable today in property values, school funding, and credit access (Rothstein, 2017).

Gentrification now threatens Black communities that survived redlining: rising property values and taxes are displacing long-term residents from neighborhoods they built and maintained for generations.

Individual Actions: Pathway to Homeownership
  • Build credit score to 680+: aim for 720+ for best mortgage rates
  • Save 3-5% of purchase price for down payment (FHA: 3.5%, conventional: 3-20%)
  • Get pre-approved with at least three lenders, including a Black-owned bank or CDFI
  • Research down payment assistance programs in your state (downpaymentresource.com)
  • Work with a HUD-approved housing counselor before purchasing (free): hud.gov/counsel
  • Consider a duplex or small multi-family property: live in one unit, rent the others
Down Payment Assistance
  • Down Payment Resource: downpaymentresource.com (search all programs by zip code)
  • HUD Home Buyer Assistance Programs: hud.gov/buying/localbuying
  • FHA loans: 3.5% down with credit score 580+
  • USDA loans (rural areas): 0% down in eligible areas
  • VA loans (veterans): 0% down, no PMI
  • State housing finance agencies: All 50 states have programs; search "[state] housing finance agency"
Anti-Displacement Strategies
  • Apply for homestead exemptions to reduce property tax (renters: advocate for rent stabilization)
  • Understand your rights as a renter: eviction moratoriums, habitability standards, anti-discrimination law
  • Community Land Trusts remove land from the speculative market permanently
  • Organize with neighbors to oppose rezoning that enables displacement
  • Participate in local planning processes before developments are approved
Community Organization Actions
  • Establish a Community Land Trust to preserve affordable homeownership long-term
  • Partner with a CDFI to create a community mortgage fund
  • Develop affordable housing on church or community-owned land
  • Create a first-time homebuyer education program
  • Advocate for community benefit agreements with developers entering the neighborhood
National Organizations

  • NAACP housing programs: naacp.org
  • NeighborWorks America: neighborworks.org
  • National Community Reinvestment Coalition: ncrc.org
  • Urban League affiliates (local): contact your local chapter

Pillar 04 / Protect More

Family & Relationships

Strong families are the foundation of strong communities. Economic stress, historical trauma, mass incarceration, and cultural disruption have placed significant strain on Black family structures. This section is not about judgment. It is about providing the resources, tools, and community infrastructure to support families in building the stability they deserve.

Individual and Family Actions
  • Pursue premarital counseling before marriage (many churches and therapists offer this)
  • Establish a family governance structure: regular family meetings, shared financial goals
  • Create a family emergency plan that everyone knows
  • Discuss family history: health, economic, and cultural history connect generations
  • Seek couples therapy at the first sign of sustained conflict, not the last
  • Model financial discipline, work ethic, and community engagement for children
Community Support Structures
  • Establish a family resource center at the church offering parenting classes, relationship support, and crisis intervention
  • Create a mentoring program pairing children without fathers or mothers with stable adult mentors
  • Build a grandparent support network for grandparents raising grandchildren
  • Develop a re-entry support program for returning citizens and their families
  • Host quarterly family engagement events to build community bonds

Pillar 04 / Protect More

Community Safety

Community safety requires addressing both interpersonal violence within communities and the documented pattern of over-policing and police-involved violence against Black Americans. This section takes both challenges seriously, because our communities deserve to be safe from all threats.

The Problem

Black Americans are killed by police at a rate more than twice that of white Americans (Mapping Police Violence, 2023). Research consistently documents that police interactions in Black neighborhoods often escalate rather than de-escalate, creating danger rather than reducing it. At the same time, concentrated poverty, inadequate investment, and lack of economic opportunity contribute to elevated rates of interpersonal violence in some Black communities. Both are legitimate safety concerns requiring different responses.

Know Your Rights: Police Encounters
  • You have the right to remain silent. State clearly: "I am invoking my right to remain silent."
  • You have the right to refuse a search without a warrant. State clearly: "I do not consent to a search."
  • You have the right to an attorney before answering questions. State clearly: "I want an attorney."
  • Record police interactions when safe to do so
  • Do not physically resist even an unlawful order; contest it in court afterward
  • Comply calmly and contest legally: the goal is to survive the encounter
  • Report misconduct to: ACLU, NAACP, your city's civilian oversight board
Gun Safety

If firearms are present in the home, gun safety is a non-negotiable responsibility. Every year, children are killed or seriously injured by unsecured firearms in the home.

  • Keep all firearms locked in a gun safe at all times when not in use
  • Keep ammunition stored separately from firearms
  • Never leave a loaded firearm accessible to children under any circumstances
  • Take a firearm safety course before purchasing
  • Teach children that real guns are never toys and that they should tell an adult immediately if they find one
  • Safe storage saves lives: Project ChildSafe: projectchildsafe.org provides free gun locks
Community Violence Prevention
  • Invest in Cure Violence and violence interruption programs: cvg.org
  • Support credible messenger programs: formerly justice-involved individuals as community mediators
  • Advocate for economic investment in high-crime areas: poverty is the root, not character
  • Establish community patrol programs in partnership with (not replacement for) residents
  • Create safe spaces and structured programming for youth during peak violence hours (3-9 PM)
  • Build a community accountability process for low-level conflicts before they escalate
Civilian Oversight Advocacy
  • Research whether your city has a civilian oversight board
  • Advocate for meaningful civilian oversight with investigatory and disciplinary powers
  • Attend police community advisory meetings and submit public comments on policing budgets
  • Organize to elect prosecutors and sheriffs who are accountable to community safety
  • Campaign Zero: joincampaignzero.org (police accountability policy platform)

Pillar 04 / Protect More

Policy & Preparedness

Policy changes at the federal, state, and local level have direct impact on every area covered in this manual: healthcare, housing, education, financial aid, and more. This section provides a framework for tracking, understanding, and responding to policy developments as they affect the Black community. Fact and analysis are clearly separated throughout.

Important Disclaimer: This section distinguishes between enacted law, executive action, agency guidance, proposed legislation, policy proposals, and commentary. These are not the same thing. Information changes rapidly. Always verify current policy status through official government sources before making financial or legal decisions based on policy.

How to Read Policy Developments

When a policy change is reported, ask these questions before reacting:

  • What is the source? Is it a law passed by Congress, an executive order, an agency regulation, or a proposal?
  • Is it enacted or proposed? Many proposals never become law.
  • What is the official text? Read primary sources at federalregister.gov, congress.gov, and whitehouse.gov.
  • What does it actually say? Headlines often mischaracterize policy scope.
  • What is the implementation timeline? Many policies phase in over years.
  • What legal challenges exist? Courts can block or modify executive actions.
Primary Sources to Monitor
  • Federal Register (executive orders, agency rules): federalregister.gov
  • Congress.gov (legislation): congress.gov
  • White House budget proposals: whitehouse.gov/omb
  • Supreme Court decisions: supremecourt.gov
  • Your state legislature: Search "[state] legislature" for bill tracking
  • USDA (agriculture, rural, food policy): usda.gov
  • HUD (housing policy): hud.gov
  • Department of Education (financial aid, student loans): ed.gov
Policy Areas to Track
  • Education: FAFSA rules, student loan programs, school funding, affirmative action
  • Healthcare: Medicaid funding, ACA provisions, maternal health programs
  • Housing: HUD programs, fair housing enforcement, Section 8 funding
  • Agriculture: USDA programs for socially disadvantaged farmers, SNAP benefits
  • Technology: AI regulation, broadband access, digital equity programs
  • Financial: CDFI Fund, Community Reinvestment Act enforcement
  • Workforce: Apprenticeship programs, job training funding
Community Response Framework

When a relevant policy change occurs, work through these response levels:

  • Individual: What does this mean for my household? What adjustments should I make?
  • Church: How can we inform and support congregation members? What programs may be affected?
  • Community organization: What advocacy is needed? What partnerships can amplify our response?
  • Legal: Are there civil rights implications? Contact NAACP Legal Defense Fund or ACLU if so.
